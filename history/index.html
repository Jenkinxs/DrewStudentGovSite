Our History

Nearly a century of student government at Drew — the fights, the reforms, and the record they left behind.

A Timeline, 1928-Present

I. Founding

  1. 1928

    Brothers College opens with 12 students on a $1.5 million gift from Arthur and Leonard Baldwin. The Drew Theological Seminary becomes Drew University.

  2. Sept 1930

    The Student Association is active on campus, hosting the opening program of the academic year.

  3. Dec 1930

    President Brown asks the college student council to formulate an Honor System before final examinations “and to organize machinery to administer it.”

  4. Jan 1931

    The student council is formally given the opportunity to study and criticize new academic regulations before their adoption.

  5. Mar 1932

    The council restricts smoking at public functions to rooms designated by the administration — and pledges to enforce its own decree. Enforcement power, not merely advice.

  6. May 1932

    A written student-body constitution is in force: an elected Student Body President, a council of four officers, and a clause requiring the council president to appoint the Freshman guide. Whitney succeeds Schroll in a closely contested presidential election.

  7. Apr 1935

    Council referenda amend the student-body constitution and advance the election date by a month.

  8. Apr 1936

    After two months of study, the Student Council drafts an Honor Code and submits it to the student body.

  9. Mar 1942

    Brothers College becomes coeducational. A new constitution is drafted: elections standardised under a supervising committee, “machine politics” targeted, and the document reworded so that both sexes fall under its jurisdiction. The old constitution, the council president says, “has been outgrown; it was set up as a club affair.”

  10. Apr 1943

    The new constitution passes the Student Council on 13 April and goes to the student body. Drafted by Spence Morris, George Mays, John Mullins, Carl Anderson and Oscar Hoffman — and defended as “a political constitution, not a club constitution.”

II. Constitution

  1. Nov 1946

    The council president amends the constitution by decree — posting it and holding a secret ballot instead of circulating it for signatures and calling a Student Association meeting, as Article IX required — then argues it is not unconstitutional because the text does not expressly forbid his method.

  2. Mar 1948

    A further new constitution goes to a student vote on 31 March.

  3. Feb 1950

    The constitution is amended by petition of 15% of the student body, adding a requirement that the council hold a mass meeting before drafting amendments. Also that month: a hazing referendum, a Commuter President seated on the council, and a standing food committee.

  4. Oct 1956

    The administration imposes a dress code in the Refectory without informing the Student Council. The Acorn protests the bypass rather than the rule.

  5. Nov 1966

    “Student Senate” is now the body’s name. The Senate presents an Open House bill to the deans backed by a petition of more than 500 signatures, and hosts the university’s Centennial Awards banquet.

III. The Senate

  1. Oct 1968

    The money architecture: ECAC — three faculty, one junior, one senior, and the Student Government President, chaired by the Dean of Students — controls $71,110 of the general fee and allots lump sums to four boards: Athletic, Academic Activities, Library, and Student Government. A referendum that autumn proposes adding three student members.

  2. 1969

    Bob Smartt is elected Student Government President in the spring. The abbreviation “SGA” enters campus usage by October.

  3. Apr 1970

    A student referendum abolishes academic grade-average eligibility requirements for office — a loophole the Judicial Board had complained it was forced to rule around.

  4. May 1970

    The Student Senate unanimously passes the General Dormitory Bill of Rights and approves a new dorm council structure, after the faculty tables the student legislative package and tells students to enact it themselves first. Judicial Board chairman George Blackford writes procedural standards guaranteeing “a hearing of grievances to every Drew college student.”

  5. Oct 1970

    The Trustees approve consumption of alcohol on campus by students of legal age — a handbook change routed through the Committee on Student Concerns and the Trustee Committee on Student Life.

  6. Dec 1970

    A new Student Association Constitution, passed by referendum, takes effect.

  7. 1972–74

    The Pub campaign. Students propose an on-campus pub in 1972; President Robert Oxnam refuses, citing discrimination against the two-thirds of students then under 21 and the risk of town opposition. New Jersey lowers the drinking age to 18 on 1 January 1973. Student Government puts the pub to a referendum in 1974 and it passes. Students incorporate the Center Pub Association and sell $1 memberships — 60 were required for a club licence. Madison’s council approves the by-laws in August; the licence follows in September.

  8. 8 Oct 1974

    The Pub opens at 4:30 p.m. The Acorn that week: “after all the talk about apathy on this campus… something has finally united the student body.”

  9. Nov 1977

    A petition demands divestment from General Electric and disclosure of all South Africa-linked holdings. President Paul Hardin refuses. Students sit in.

IV. Divestment

  1. Feb 1978

    The SGA Committee on Drew’s Investment Responsibilities raises a small sum — the OCR reads “nearly $50” — to buy stock in U.S. corporations in the student association’s name, so that students can vote shareholder resolutions directly on apartheid, environmental and disclosure questions.

  2. Dec 1978

    The Investment Responsibility Committee is reviewing shareholder resolutions before the Trustees consider them; the SGA and Senate approve its recommendations first.

  3. Mar 1985

    A student referendum asking the Trustees to divest from all corporations doing business in South Africa “easily passed,” though the Acorn notes it was strangely worded.

  4. Oct 1985

    The SGA passes its own divestment resolution. President Stephen Foster presents it to the Board of Trustees in person.

  5. Dec 1985

    The Board refuses full divestment, adopting only a Sullivan Principles screen. Students call it “a minor and unencouraging” first step.

  6. 1986

    The Drew Anti-Apartheid Movement forms. A day-long teach-in on 21 March marks Sharpeville. In April, 65 protesters with black balloons and candles line the trustees’ meeting and read a Divestment Declaration. In October, more than 130 march on Mead Hall.

  7. Oct 1987

    The Trustees adopt a partial policy: no new stock in corporations doing business in South Africa, and divestment within a year of any holding without a top-two Sullivan rating. A Social Responsibility Committee is formed that winter.

  8. Mar 1989

    The Trustees declare the portfolio “totally divested” of South Africa-linked holdings, with the exception of one pharmaceutical company.

  9. Nov 1990

    The Board broadens the policy again. The Acorn calls it “one of the most far-reaching negotiated policies toward divestment.”

  10. Apr 1991

    Activists note the university has still not divested completely. It never did so cleanly — but the campaign moved an endowment.

V.

  1. Feb 1992

    SGA President Sodan Selvaretnam’s constitutional committee, meeting weekly for over an hour a session, proposes a new constitution. Restructuring dominates the year.

  2. May 2010

    SGA President Nelson Melendez signs the national student-presidents’ letter on clean energy legislation.

VI. Transparency

  1. 14 Feb 2018

    Weekly public summaries of Senate meetings begin appearing on Facebook, a transparency initiative of President Salma Mahmoud and Vice President Shehab Marzouk, run by Chief Communications Officer Nicole Albornoz.

  2. Apr 2018

    After the defacement of a Black Lives Matter sign, the Senate passes a motion to plan a CLA-centred event and issue a statement, under pressure from students led by former Senator Richard Taylor.

  3. Mar 2021

    A COVID Concerns committee is formed. The Senate Secretary role expands into public introductions of every senator and cabinet member on Instagram.

  4. Dec 2021

    Nate Roark and Andrew Lora are inaugurated President and Vice President on 12 December, and resurrect the Constitutional Revision Committee.

  5. 2022

    The Textbook Lending Library launches, a need-based project of Senator Gabriella Ramirez. On 23 February the Senate unanimously passes a resolution, written by Senators Gabby Rizzolo and Rachel Papa, demanding transparency from President Thomas Schwarz over the potential sale of the Drew Forest.

  6. Dec 2023

    One presidential ticket runs and wins with 47 votes. A former Chief of Staff later writes that senators routinely won with barely a third of their class voting, and that cabinets had to appoint senators rather than swear in elected ones.

  7. 13 Mar 2024

    President Brazil Brown is impeached on a charge of failing to comply with the constitution’s requirement that the president serve as spokesperson for the student body. She had offered to resign; the Senate chose impeachment and elevated Vice President Julia Satola to the presidency.

  8. Sept 2024

    An act passes lowering the cost of student health insurance.

  9. Nov 2024

    Commuter Senator Maks Coven secures a full-size refrigerator for the reopened Commuter Lounge after storm damage closed it for the semester.

  10. Apr 2025

    BOB concludes budget interviews for more than 40 clubs against campus-wide cuts. Across the year the board fields $182,000 in requests and allocates roughly $90,000 to about 60 clubs.

  11. Sept 2025

    A permanent Transfer Senator seat is created; Dejanayha Hill is its first holder. Zaire Wright and Daniel Ohr lead the Senate.

  12. Nov 2025

    A town hall commits the administration to a designated campus mental health day and to under-21 access to the Pub at set times. A free period-products initiative is debated the month before.

  13. Jan 2026

    The Drew Forest is sold to the Borough of Madison — preserved, but by municipal purchase rather than by the university conceding the students’ 2022 demands.

  14. Mar 2026

    Commuter Senator Thomas Jenkins launches a 24/7 Student Complaints and Concerns hotline on Google Voice, with anonymised transcripts reviewed by senators.

  15. Sept 2026

    The fall Senate election is invalidated and rerun on 25 September. Nine of twelve seats had been vacant; eight are filled. The reform coalition Students for Change wins six of them, taking the majority of the Guzman–Jenkins Senate.
